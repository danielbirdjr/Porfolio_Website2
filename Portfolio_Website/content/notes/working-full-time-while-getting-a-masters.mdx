People ask how I fit a full-time engineering job, a master's at Georgia Tech, and lifting into the same week. The honest answer is: I don't rely on motivation. I rely on systems that make the default behavior the right one.

## Energy, not time, is the constraint

Everyone has the same 168 hours. What runs out isn't time — it's focus. So I protect my best hours ruthlessly. My sharpest thinking happens in the morning, so that block goes to the hardest thing on my plate: deep work at my job or a difficult problem set. Email, errands, and admin get the dregs of my attention in the afternoon, when I'd be useless at anything harder.

> Spend your peak hours on your hardest work. Everything else can run on autopilot.

## Default schedules beat daily decisions

Deciding *when* to study or train every day is a tax. I pay it once, up front, by building a repeating weekly template:

- Mornings: deep work
- Lunch: train (it's non-negotiable and on the calendar like a meeting)
- Evenings: coursework, three nights a week
- One full rest day with zero obligations

Once it's a default, I'm not spending willpower deciding — I'm just following the plan.

## Make the trade-offs explicit

You cannot do everything at maximum intensity at once. During finals, training volume drops and that's fine. During a big work deadline, coursework gets the minimum. The skill isn't doing it all — it's knowing which ball is made of glass this week and which ones can bounce.

## Rest is part of the work

The fastest way to wreck all three goals at once is to stop sleeping. I treat sleep as the foundation the rest is built on, not the thing I sacrifice when I'm behind. Being behind and exhausted is strictly worse than being behind and rested.

## The compounding payoff

None of this is glamorous. It's just showing up to a system, day after day, and letting small consistent inputs compound. That's the same lesson the gym teaches — and it's why the people who win long-term are rarely the most talented. They're the most consistent.
